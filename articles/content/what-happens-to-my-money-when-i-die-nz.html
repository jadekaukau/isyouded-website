Good news first: your loved ones don't inherit your debts. Your money, and your debts, become your estate, and your estate settles up before anyone gets a cent.

Bad news: if nobody knows what you've got, who you owe or where any of it lives, someone you love spends months finding out. So here's what actually happens to your money in New Zealand when you die, from the student loan to the KiwiSaver.

First, meet your estate

Your estate is everything you own in your own name when you die: bank accounts, KiwiSaver, your car, your share of a house, that drawer of cash you swear isn't a drawer of cash. It's also everything you owe.

The person in charge of it is your executor (the one you named in your will) or, if there's no will, an administrator the High Court appoints. Their job is to gather it all up, pay the bills, then hand out what's left.

Once your bank hears you've died, accounts in your sole name are frozen. Nothing goes in or out until your executor shows the right paperwork. Joint accounts are different: they usually pass straight to the other account holder.

Your student loan: it dies with you

Yes, really. If you die with a student loan, Inland Revenue writes it off. Your estate doesn't pay it, your parents don't pay it, your partner doesn't pay it.

IR just needs to be told. Your executor sends IR proof of death and proof they can act for you, and IR does the rest. The only catch: any repayments that were already due before you died still come out of your estate.

About 600,000 Kiwis have a student loan, so this is one less thing for a lot of us to haunt people about.

Credit cards, loans and Afterpay: the estate pays

Everything else you owe gets paid out of your estate, before anything goes to the people in your will. That includes credit cards, personal loans, car finance, buy now pay later and the power bill. Funeral costs and the cost of sorting out the estate come first.

If there isn't enough to cover it all, the debt doesn't jump to your family. Debts in your name alone stay with your estate. The lender may have to write off what's left.

The exceptions, where someone else can be on the hook:

  • Joint debts. A joint loan, joint credit card or joint mortgage still belongs to the other person.
  • Guarantors. If someone guaranteed your loan, they can be asked to pay it.

The mortgage is secured on the house, so it has to be paid. That might mean selling the house, unless the other owner takes it over or there's insurance to pay it off.

Your KiwiSaver: it goes to your estate

For a lot of us, KiwiSaver is the biggest thing we own outright. When you die, it doesn't go straight to your partner. You can't nominate a beneficiary either. It becomes part of your estate, and your executor claims it from your provider.

Who gets it then depends on your will. No will? The law decides for you, using a set formula, and that might not match what you'd want. A partner may also have a claim to it as relationship property.

The rest of your money, and the $40,000 rule

To release your money, banks and KiwiSaver providers usually want to see probate: a High Court document confirming your will and your executor. It takes time and often a lawyer.

Since 24 September 2025, smaller amounts can skip that step. A bank, KiwiSaver provider or insurer can release up to $40,000 they hold for you without probate (it used to be $15,000). It's their call, not a guarantee, and it doesn't cover a house in your name.

The catch: the limit applies to each organisation's total, and your estate still has to be small overall. $20,000 at one bank, $15,000 at another and $25,000 in KiwiSaver is a $60,000 estate, so probate is likely still needed.

What you can do now

You can't take it with you, but you can make it a lot easier to find.

  1. Make a will. It names your executor and says who gets your KiwiSaver and everything else. Without one, the law picks.
  2. List what you've got. Every bank, your KiwiSaver provider, insurance, shares, crypto, the lot. No logins or PINs needed, just where it lives.
  3. List what you owe. Loans, cards, buy now pay later, and anyone who's guaranteed something for you.
  4. Tell someone where the list is. Or better, keep it somewhere they'll actually find it.
