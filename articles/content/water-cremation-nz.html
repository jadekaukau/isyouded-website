Until recently, if you died in New Zealand, it was pretty much burial or cremation, plus a few less common options like natural burial or donating your body to science. Now there's another. Water cremation arrived in Aotearoa in June 2025, and the Christchurch funeral home that offers it says it's already proving popular with families.

The short version. Water cremation uses water, a little alkali, heat and pressure instead of fire. Your family still gets ashes back. It's available in Christchurch, with more places on the way. If you want it, write it down so your people know.

What is it?

Water cremation goes by a few names: aquamation, alkaline hydrolysis or resomation. They all mean the same thing.

Instead of fire, the body is gently broken down in a mix of about 95% water and 5% alkali (potassium hydroxide), heated to around 150°C under pressure, so it never actually boils. It takes about four hours. It's the same natural process that happens in the ground over many years, just much faster.

What happens on the day

  • The person is wrapped in a natural shroud (in Christchurch, New Zealand wool). There's no casket, though you can still have one for the funeral itself.
  • They're placed in a stainless steel machine called a resomator.
  • Your family can be in the room to say goodbye, and can even help wrap your person and place them in the machine. That's not possible with flame cremation because of the heat.
  • After about four hours, what's left is the bones and any metal implants, like a hip replacement. Pacemakers don't need to be removed first, as they do for a flame cremation. The metal is recycled.

Do you still get ashes?

Yes. The bones are dried and processed into a fine, white powder, just like the ashes from a flame cremation. There's usually 20 to 30% more of it, and it's white rather than grey. You can scatter them, keep them, bury them or turn them into jewellery: all the same options.

What happens to the water?

This is the bit people always ask about. What's left is a sterile liquid of amino acids, sugars and salts. It's neutralised and returned to the water cycle through the wastewater system, with the council's permission. In Christchurch, the provider worked with mana whenua and had independent reports done before it opened.

Is it legal in New Zealand?

Yes, where the local council allows it. Our main law on this, the Burial and Cremation Act, dates from 1964 and only mentions burial and flame cremation. It doesn't say anything about water cremation either way, so it's up to councils to approve it in their area. Christchurch City Council has.

The law is overdue for an update, and the funeral industry has been pushing for change, so this could become clearer in the next few years.

Where can you get it?

  • Christchurch: New Zealand's first water crematorium opened on 6 June 2025 at Bell, Lamb and Trotter.
  • Hamilton and Auckland: facilities are in the works, but not open yet, according to the Funeral Directors Association.

If you live somewhere else, it may still be possible. Your funeral director can talk to the Christchurch crematorium about transport, which will add to the cost.

What does it cost?

When it launched in June 2025, the water cremation itself was priced at $1,350. The provider said that was slightly cheaper than a flame cremation, partly because you don't need a casket. Funeral director fees, a service and any transport are on top, just like with any cremation. Here are some ballpark figures for the whole thing, based on Christchurch funeral packages in 2026:

What you're afterBallpark
Simple water cremation, no serviceAbout $5,500
A small gathering in a chapel, plus water cremationAbout $6,800
Water cremation first, memorial service afterwardsAbout $8,500
A full funeral service, plus water cremation$10,000–$14,000

For comparison, a simple flame cremation with no service is around $2,000 to $3,400 from a budget provider, and the funeral industry's 2026 average is about $4,700. A full funeral with a flame cremation averages around $11,700. So water cremation lands in a similar range: the bigger costs are the funeral director, the venue and the service, not the cremation itself.

These are rough figures to help you plan, not quotes. If you're not in Christchurch, add the cost of transport.

Ask your funeral director for a quote. If money's tight, cremation fees count towards Work and Income's Funeral Grant for families who are eligible. It's worth checking with them that a water cremation is covered.

Is it really greener?

The providers say water cremation uses about 90% less energy than flame cremation and doesn't release mercury from dental fillings into the air. Those are the providers' own figures, but the basic idea holds up: no fire, no fuel and no casket being burned means a much smaller footprint.

Things to think about

  • Culture and faith. Some traditions have strong views on what happens to the body. Talk it through with your whānau, iwi or faith leader.
  • Size. Water cremation machines currently take people up to about 150kg.
  • Where you'll be. If you're not in Christchurch, think about whether you're happy for your body to travel there, or whether you'd rather wait and see if it comes closer.
  • Your people need to know. Water cremation is new, so your family might not even know it's an option. If it's what you want, write it down.

How to choose it

  1. Decide it's what you want, and tell your people.
  2. Write it in your plan (DED. has a spot for exactly this) or your will, so it's not left to guesswork.
  3. When the time comes, your people ask their funeral director to arrange a water cremation.
  4. The ashes are returned to the family, just like a flame cremation.
